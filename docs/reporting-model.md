# S.I.R. Observation Reporting Model

## Purpose

Every link in the knowledge chain is now settled except this one. Perception
produces local observations, communications carry them, electronic warfare
attacks the carriage, and command bandwidth prices the enrichment. What has
remained provisional since the initial vision is the rule deciding **which
observations become reports at all** — and therefore what information exists to
flow.

This is the vision's third open question, and it is the last authoritative gap
in the information model.

## Why fixed reports exist when players can already send anything

The obvious objection first, because it determines the shape of everything else.

A module can already transmit whatever it likes as a player-defined payload. So
why does an authoritative report system exist alongside that?

**Because it is the floor, not a competitor.** Fixed reports are the information
flow that works for a player who has written no protocol and no module. Without
them, a player using the standard module would be blind, and authorship would be
mandatory in practice while the documents claimed otherwise.

That settles the relationship: **reports are the guaranteed baseline, and
player messaging is the optimisation on top of it.** A sophisticated author can
carry the same intelligence more densely in their own protocol. They cannot
carry *less* than nothing, and they gain nothing by reimplementing what the
baseline already provides.

It also settles why modules cannot suppress, invent, or modify report facts, as
the vision already requires. A floor a module could remove is not a floor.

## Reports are generated by events, not by state

**A report describes a change, not a condition.** A unit does not report that it
still sees three hostiles; it reports that it has gained a contact, and later
that one has been lost.

This is the single most consequential rule here, and it does three things at
once:

- it bounds traffic to the **rate of change** rather than the rate of
  observation, which is what keeps reporting affordable at a hundred units per
  side;
- it means a static situation is a quiet one, so silence is informative rather
  than merely absent; and
- it reuses machinery that already exists. The same authoritative events that
  reach a unit's own module — contact gained, contact lost, damage taken,
  ammunition crossing a threshold, an engagement solution lost — are the
  candidates for forwarding upward.

A report is therefore a *forwarded event with provenance*, not a separate
category of thing the simulation must invent.

## Significance decides what is forwarded

Not every event deserves a transmission. Each event class carries an
authoritative **significance**, and a reporting threshold decides what clears
the bar.

Indicative tiers, with exact assignment as prototype data:

| Significance | Examples |
|---|---|
| High | First contact with a previously unknown hostile; casualties; contact with a new class of threat; loss of a command element |
| Medium | A tracked contact lost; ammunition below reserve; arrival at a referent; formation broken |
| Low | Position refinement on a contact already reported; routine status; stance changes |

The threshold is not fixed by the simulation. It is set by the unit's posture
and overrides, which is where a commander decides how talkative an element
should be — see [Standard Module](standard-module.md).

## Aggregation happens at every hop

A squad leader does not forward twelve individual contact reports. They forward
one: *squad in contact, estimated strength, bearing.*

**Aggregation is the information work the command hierarchy exists to do**, and
making it explicit produces several things the design wants:

- traffic does not grow linearly with force size, which is what makes a hundred
  units per side reportable at all;
- a squad leader becomes an information node whose value is legible, which is
  consistent with traffic analysis already identifying convergence nodes as
  worth killing; and
- **losing a leader does not merely sever a link, it removes the thing that made
  a squad's information legible.** Headquarters does not fall back to raw feeds;
  it gets nothing until command is re-established.

Aggregation costs latency, because a summary must wait for something to
summarise. That is a real trade and belongs to posture: an element told to
report immediately is faster and louder, one told to summarise is slower and
quieter.

## What a report carries

As already established, a report carries factual metadata rather than certainty
labels: observation time, observer, sensor and provenance, and whatever the
observation itself supports. The client and the player decide what it means.

Provenance survives aggregation. A summary states what it was derived from and
when its oldest constituent was observed, because a summary whose age is unknown
is worse than no summary.

## Transport delay and report ordering

The command network applies the same canonical one-second-per-leg delay to
reports, observations, orders, acknowledgements, status updates, and
player-defined traffic. Reports receive no faster upstream path. A member's
local report can reach its leader after the one-tick squad-net minimum, but
every subsequent command-net leg costs 20 ticks.

Headquarters and the player therefore receive a delayed command picture rather
than privileged live state. Each delivered report carries at least:

- its observation tick;
- its arrival tick;
- the original observer and sensor;
- its report or event identity;
- its provenance through aggregation; and
- the command route or hop count needed to explain its age.

Messages preserve order on one unchanged link, but different routes, queues, or
reconnections can cause a newer observation to arrive before an older one. An
older observation that arrives later is retained as history and cannot
overwrite knowledge from a newer observation of the same event or contact.
Reports from independent observers may conflict and remain separate evidence;
the server does not manufacture a single reconciled truth.

The interface must expose information age and provenance rather than presenting
delayed data as current. Sending an order can be shown immediately, but receipt,
execution, or completion becomes known only through an acknowledgement that
travels back through the delayed network. Units evaluate an order against their
current local situation when it arrives; delivery never rewinds simulation
state.

## Reports cost what everything else costs

Reports share link capacity with player payloads and emit exactly as any other
traffic does. A unit that reports heavily is a unit that is easy to find.

They are **not** drawn from allocated command bandwidth. Allocation prices what
a commander gives a unit, not what a unit tells its commander — see
[Communications Network](communications-network.md). Upstream is bounded by
capacity, by aggregation, and above all by emission.

This is the trade that makes reporting policy a decision rather than a setting:
**report volume trades information against signature.** It is emission control
applied to the generation of traffic rather than to its transmission, and it is
genuinely undetermined because the right answer depends on whether the element
is trying to remain hidden.

A screening element that reports every position refinement is not screening. An
assaulting element that reports nothing is wasting a link it is already paying
for by being visible anyway.

## Disconnection, retention, and reconnection

Reports that cannot be delivered are retained under the store-and-forward rules,
bounded by depth and expiry.

On reconnection there is a choice, and it should be a policy rather than a
default: **send the retained log, or send a current summary.**

- the **log** preserves history, including contacts that have since been lost,
  and is what an analyst would want; while
- the **summary** is smaller, fresher, and produces a far less conspicuous
  reconnection burst.

Because a reconnection burst is one of the most legible events available to
traffic analysis, summarising on reconnection is also emission control. A squad
re-establishing contact under observation has a genuine reason to say less.

## What a module may and may not do

A module **may**:

- set the reporting threshold and aggregation policy through its configuration
  and through posture;
- send anything additional it likes as a player-defined payload; and
- decide what to do with reports it receives.

A module **may not**:

- suppress an authoritative report that clears the threshold;
- invent a report, or alter the facts or provenance of one; or
- report facts its unit does not legitimately know.

The boundary is that a module controls **how talkative** its unit is, not
**whether the truth reaches its own commander**.

## Checking this against invariant 13

No reporting policy is unconditionally correct.

Reporting everything costs capacity and signature, and produces a force that can
be mapped from its traffic alone. Reporting nothing blinds the
commander and starves the aggregation the hierarchy depends on. Summarising
trades latency for volume, and immediate reporting trades volume for speed.

The right setting differs by element, by posture, by whether the element is
concealed, and by whether the opponent is equipped to exploit volume. That is
the condition invariant 13 requires.

## Failure modes to avoid

- Reporting driven by state rather than change, which scales with force size
  rather than with events and saturates at the intended scale.
- A report system a module can silence, which makes it not a floor.
- Aggregation that discards provenance or age, producing summaries a commander
  cannot judge.
- Reports exempt from capacity, bandwidth, or emission costs, which would make
  maximal reporting free and therefore universal.
- Certainty labels attached by the server, which the vision already rejects in
  favour of factual metadata.
- Making the player-defined channel strictly better than the floor in every
  respect, which would make protocol authorship mandatory by the back door.

## Open parameters

- Significance assignment per event class.
- Threshold levels, and how many a posture needs to distinguish.
- Aggregation windows, and whether they are time-based or count-based.
- Whether aggregation is performed by the unit, the leader, or both.
- How a summary represents uncertainty in strength and composition.
- Retention depth and expiry for undeliverable reports.
- Whether the log-versus-summary reconnection choice is per-posture or global.
- Whether headquarters can request a report on demand, and what that costs.
